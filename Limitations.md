# Limitations of the Policy

**Task 7: The Ethics of Synthetic Representation**

An honest account of where the policy in phase_b_policy.md would fail, what it cannot guarantee, and what residual risk remains after adoption.

---

## Purpose of This Section

This section is not an apology for the policy. It is a demonstration that I understand what the policy has and has not accomplished. A policy that pretends to guarantee outcomes it cannot guarantee is worse than one that admits its limits, because the first produces complacency and the second produces vigilance. What follows is a candid account of the gaps.

---

## 1. The Policy Cannot Control What Happens After Distribution

The policy requires in artifact disclosure, embedded metadata, and provenance credentials on all synthetic media the office produces. It cannot prevent any of those from being stripped once the file leaves the office's control.

A fan account can download a disclosed video, crop the watermark, re encode the file through a mobile editing app, and post it with no disclosure at all. Platform compression routinely discards metadata. Screenshotting and screen recording eliminate provenance entirely. The policy's disclosure standards work only inside the channels the office controls. Outside those channels, the disclosure travels zero hops while the artifact travels many.

This is a structural limitation, not a fixable one. The policy acknowledges it in Section 5.4 and Section 8.2, but acknowledging it does not solve it. The residual risk is that content produced ethically by the office can be repurposed unethically by third parties, and the office will have no recourse.

---

## 2. The Policy Depends on Good Faith at Every Enforcement Point

The policy assumes that staff will submit proposals to the review board before production begins. It assumes the consent coordinator will obtain written consent for every applicable use. It assumes the disclosure will actually be included in the exported artifact. It assumes incidents will be reported within twenty four hours.

None of these assumptions is enforced in real time. A staff member who wants to cut a corner can produce synthetic media on a personal device, on a personal account, and never submit it to the review board. The policy has no technical mechanism to prevent this. It relies on professional norms, supervision, and the employee's own judgment.

This reliance on good faith is not a fatal weakness. Every policy that governs human behavior relies on good faith to some degree. But it is an honest weakness, and it means the policy is only as strong as the culture of the office that adopts it. A well intentioned employee who is rushed, or who misreads a boundary, or who assumes an informal exception, can produce a violation without ever intending one.

---

## 3. The Consent Workflow Has Real Gaps

Section 4 requires written, signed, dated consent specific to the proposed use. This is strong on paper. In practice, several gaps remain.

First, consent can be obtained under pressure. A junior staff member asking a senior coach to sign a form may not feel empowered to explain what the coach is actually authorizing. The form is specific, but the conversation that surrounds it may not be.

Second, revocation is only effective on channels the office controls. If content has already been downloaded and re shared, revocation cannot recall it. The consent form says this, but a person revoking consent may not fully understand it until the harm has occurred.

Third, consent for archival reconstruction under Section 2.4 requires approval from the estate of a deceased person. Estates are not always clearly defined, and disputes among family members can delay or complicate the process. The policy does not address what to do when the estate is contested.

Fourth, the policy does not define what happens when a person consents to one use and then objects to a different use that emerges downstream. A coach who consents to an accessibility narration may later object to the same audio being used in a promotional video. The policy does not have a clear process for that scenario.

---

## 4. The Review Board Is a Bottleneck and a Blind Spot

Section 6 requires review board approval for any use outside Sections 2.1 and 2.2, and it requires five business days of lead time. In a fast moving athletics communications environment, five business days is a long time. The predictable result is that staff will route around the review board when deadlines are tight, either by classifying their use as an accessibility use under Section 2.1 or by not asking at all.

The review board is also only three people. Its legal counsel may not be a specialist in synthetic media law. Its rotating staff member may not have technical expertise. The board is asked to evaluate proposals across a wide range of use cases, and it will likely develop informal heuristics rather than rigorous standards. Its decisions may be inconsistent over time.

A single objection under Section 8.7 can pause a project indefinitely. This is a strong protection against bad uses, but it is also a potential tool for internal politics. A board member with an unrelated grievance could use the objection process to delay work they dislike. The policy does not have a mechanism for resolving frivolous objections quickly.

---

## 5. The Prohibitions Are Clear but Not Enforceable Against Outsiders

Section 3 prohibits a range of uses outright. Those prohibitions bind the office and its staff. They do not bind anyone else.

A rival program, a fan account, a recruiting service, or a foreign actor can produce synthetic media depicting this university's coaches, athletes, or brand without any regard for this policy. The policy cannot stop them. It can only ensure that the office itself does not contribute to the problem.

This is the most important limitation to state plainly. The policy governs the office. It does not govern the ecosystem. It is a unilateral commitment in a multilateral problem, and its value depends on other actors making similar commitments, which the office cannot compel.

---

## 6. Detection and Verification Are Not Solved

The policy requires content credentials when the production tool supports them, and metadata embedded in the file name and properties. It does not require the office to verify that synthetic media it encounters from outside sources is authentic before responding to it.

If a reporter calls the office about a synthetic video of the coach that is circulating, the office has no internal capability to verify whether the video is synthetic or real. The policy does not provide for detection tools, forensic analysis, or a relationship with a verification service. The office may be forced to respond to a synthetic artifact without being able to determine what it is.

This is a gap in the policy's scope. It addresses production but not reception. A more complete policy would include a verification protocol for inbound synthetic media, and the resources to execute it.

---

## 7. The Policy Does Not Address Internal Use of Personal Tools

The policy applies to content produced on behalf of the office. It does not address staff who use synthetic tools on personal accounts, personal devices, or in personal capacities.

A staff member who produces a synthetic video of a coach on their own time and posts it to their personal social media has technically not violated this policy, because the policy governs office production. But the artifact may still carry the university's brand, still depict a person associated with the university, and still cause harm. The policy does not reach this scenario, and the office may not have the authority to regulate it.

This is a boundary problem, not an oversight. Extending the policy to personal conduct raises serious questions about employee rights and university authority. The policy chooses not to cross that line. The choice is defensible, but the residual risk is real.

---

## 8. The Policy Relies on Definitions That Will Erode

Section 1 defines synthetic media, identifiable characteristics, and institutional identity. These definitions are clear today. They will become less clear as the technology evolves.

When a video editing tool uses AI to remove a background, is that synthetic media? When a photo editing tool uses AI to sharpen a face, is that synthetic media? When a large language model drafts a press release that a human then edits, is that synthetic media? The policy does not answer these questions, and a strict reading would prohibit routine uses that no one considers problematic.

The definitions will need to be revisited as tools change. The policy's eighteen month sunset clause in Section 9.4 provides a mechanism for that revision, but eighteen months is a long time in a field that changes monthly. Between reviews, staff will face ambiguous cases and will resolve them by judgment. Some of those judgments will be wrong.

---

## 9. Where the Policy Depends on Good Faith, and Whether That Is Fatal

The honest summary is that this policy depends on good faith at almost every point. It depends on staff submitting proposals, obtaining consent, applying disclosure, reporting incidents, and respecting prohibitions. It depends on the review board applying standards consistently. It depends on the Director enforcing the policy. It depends on the university supporting the office when enforcement is inconvenient.

Is that reliance fatal. No. Every governance document depends on good faith to some degree, because no document can compel behavior it cannot observe. The alternative to a good faith policy is no policy at all, which leaves the office with no shared standard and no process for accountability.

But the reliance is significant, and the policy should not be presented as a guarantee. It is a commitment. It sets expectations, creates process, and gives the office a basis for accountability when expectations are not met. That is what a policy can do. It cannot do more.

---

## 10. What a Stronger Policy Would Need

For completeness, a stronger policy would need the following, none of which this policy provides.

A technical enforcement mechanism that attaches disclosure and provenance at the point of production in a way that cannot be stripped by the producer, which no current tool reliably provides.

A detection and verification capability for inbound synthetic media, which requires resources and expertise the office does not have.

A cross institutional agreement among peer universities and athletic conferences to adopt parallel policies, which would reduce the unilateral disadvantage of being the only program that refuses certain uses.

A legal framework that provides recourse when synthetic media causes harm, which currently exists only in fragments and varies by jurisdiction.

A culture of ethical seriousness that survives staff turnover, budget pressure, and the next exciting tool that promises to make all of this easier. Which is to say, a culture that has to be rebuilt every year.

---

## Closing

This policy is honest about what it can do. It can set clear boundaries, establish process, and create accountability inside the office. It cannot control the ecosystem, cannot compel outsiders, cannot guarantee that its own staff will always comply, and cannot survive contact with the next generation of tools without revision. It is a starting point, not a solution. The residual risk is real, and the office that adopts this policy should adopt it with open eyes about what it is and is not.
